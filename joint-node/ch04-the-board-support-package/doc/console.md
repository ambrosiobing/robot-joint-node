# The console: the registers, the divisor, and why it must print its own clock

Chapter 4's image floor, third piece, Sunday 25 October 2026. The map says where
things go and the startup gets the part to `main`. This is what `main` needs
before it can say anything.

## Settled by the board's own device tree

`[vendor]` Zephyr `boards/st/nucleo_h7a3zi_q/nucleo_h7a3zi_q.dts`, whose chosen
node names `usart3` and whose pinctrl names `usart3_tx_pd8` and
`usart3_rx_pd9`: **USART3, PD8 transmit, PD9 receive, 115200.**

**One finding from that belongs to chapter 9.** The console is on `PD8` and
`PD9`; chapter 9's bus is on `PD0` and `PD1`. One port, two peripherals, one
shared clock enable. An image that configures either by writing whole port
registers rather than by read, modify, write takes the other's pins with it, and
the symptom is a console that stops or a bus that goes quiet with nothing
reported.

**That NuttX header is named for the H743, not this part.** Chapter 9 already
established that this part's clock selector registers differ in NAME from the
sibling's, so reading the sibling's header is exactly the mistake this volume
has a note about. What makes it admissible here is that NuttX itself maps
`CONFIG_STM32_STM32H7B3XX` to that same file, and the H7B3 is this part's own
family rather than the H743 line. The bit numbers are also corroborated
independently: Zephyr's device tree gives AHB4 bit 3 and APB1 bit 18 without
reference to NuttX at all.

It is still `[vendor]` reasoning rather than a reading of this part's own
manual, and that has a design consequence. **The console's setup reads its
enable bits back**, because a console that is silent because of a wrong
register offset looks exactly like a console that is silent for any of a dozen
other reasons, and a read-back is the difference between a report and a guess.

## A console cannot check its own baud

Chapter 10's lesson about a serial loopback applies here in a harder form. A
loopback shares its clock with itself, so it passes at any rate and proves
nothing about the bit rate. This console has no loopback at all: it writes into a
transmitter and never hears back.

So the instrument is the **terminal**, a PC running at a known fixed rate, and
the board is the thing being measured. The only evidence available is that the
characters received are the characters sent.

Which is why **the first image prints the clock it assumed and the divisor it
computed.** Legible output then confirms the assumption. Illegible output says
which assumption to doubt, and without those numbers printed it says nothing at
all.

## The kernel clock, bounded on Thursday 12 November 2026

`[measured]` USART3's kernel clock comes from a selector whose reset value is
the APB1 peripheral clock, and what that is after reset on this part had not
been read. The sibling's reset values are not evidence, for the reason above.

**It is now bounded, by the first image that prints.** 5247 bytes arrived
legibly at 115200 8N1, with the divisor at 556 and the prescaler at 1. The
actual rate is therefore `F_kernel / 556` and the receiver decoded it, so at a
conservative two per cent receiver tolerance `F_kernel` lies between 62.77 and
65.33 MHz, and even at the theoretical limit of half a bit time of accumulated
error at the stop bit of a ten bit frame it lies between 60.68 and 67.42 MHz.

**The prescaler chain is 1:1 after reset:** 32 MHz and 128 MHz are both excluded
by roughly a factor of two, and that was the whole question. What is NOT
established is that the clock is exactly 64.000 MHz, nor anything about the
oscillator's trim. A ratio was bounded; no frequency was measured.

## The arithmetic, and two things it would have been easy to get wrong

`[kernel]` From the mainline driver, `drivers/tty/serial/stm32-usart.c` and
`stm32-usart.h`, read on Sunday 25 October 2026.

**The register word is composed as a mantissa shifted by four with the remainder
in the low four bits, and at 16 times oversampling that is an identity.**
`USART_BRR_DIV_M_SHIFT` is 4, so `((d / 16) << 4) | (d % 16)` is exactly `d`.
The split only does work at 8 times. Writing the composition out and assuming it
matters would have been harmless; assuming the opposite, that `BRR` is something
other than the divisor, would not. The rule asserts the identity across every
divisor from 16 to 4095 rather than trusting this paragraph.

**The divisor is rounded to nearest, not truncated.** At 64 MHz and 115200 that
is the difference between 556 and 555, and between 0.8 and 1.9 per thousand of
error. A rule checks both halves: that the function is never more than half a
count from the true quotient, and that at least one real case would have come
out differently under truncation. Without the second half the rounding rule
would be untested and the claim that it matters unsupported.

## What the checks proved, including a rule about the rules

Two mutations, each with the vectors regenerated to agree so only the rules
could object:

| Mutation | What objected |
|---|---|
| truncate instead of rounding to nearest | the half-count bound, and the rule that truncation must change at least one real case |
| remove the case that trips the error limit | the reachability rule below |

**And the rule that found its own gap.** Five refusal reasons are written in the
solver, and when they were first checked only three were reachable. The case
meant to trip the error limit was refused by the under-16 divisor rule before it
got there, and the exhaustion path at the end of the prescaler loop had no case
at all. A refusal nothing reaches is a claim about behaviour rather than
behaviour.

There is now a rule requiring every refusal reason to be reached by some case,
and two cases chosen to reach the two that were not: 100 kbit from 1.65 MHz,
whose divisor is a legal 17 and whose rounding error alone is 29.4 per thousand
against a limit of 25, and 1 baud from 500 MHz, where even the 256 prescaler
leaves the divisor past sixteen bits.

## The code, Friday 6 November 2026

`src/bsp/console.c` exists, and `src/bsp/baud.c` carries the arithmetic above
separately so that it can be checked on a host against the same vectors. Five
things in the console go wrong quietly, and each has a rule that was proved able
to fail by writing the mistake and watching the rule name it.

| Mistake | What happens | The rule |
|---|---|---|
| a plain write to `MODER`, `OSPEEDR` or `PUPDR` | one word covers all sixteen pins of port D, two of which are chapter 9's bus, so the other fourteen take whatever the expression says; a console that works while having returned `PD0` and `PD1` to inputs is the worst outcome available | every write to those registers must derive from the register's own prior value, and `init` snapshots the non-owned bits and refuses with `pins-disturbed` if any moved |
| an enable bit that did not take | a peripheral with no clock accepts every write and reads back zeroes, which is the same picture as a working console nobody is listening to | both enable bits are set and read back, which matters because their offsets are `[vendor]` reasoning from a sibling-named header |
| clearing the status flags after the byte | `TC` left set by the previous character makes a later flush return at once, before this byte has gone anywhere | the clear must appear before the `TDR` write, checked by position |
| `BRR` written to a running peripheral | the divisor has no write guarantee while the USART is enabled, and one that half takes is a rate nobody computed | `CR1` must be cleared before `BRR`, checked by position |
| an unbounded wait on a flag | a blank console and no clue, indistinguishable from a part that never started | every wait is counted against a bound, and a bound of zero is refused |

`AFRL` and `AFRH` split at pin 8, so the alternate-function writes cannot reach
`PD0` or `PD1` at all. That is a property of where the split falls rather than
care taken in the code, and it is written down because the next peripheral on
this port may not be so lucky.

**Every function returns a bool.** A void putchar cannot report that the
transmitter never became ready, and its caller would print a whole banner into
nothing. The same rule made `baud_solve` return a verdict rather than a bare
false: the generator refuses five different ways and each one has to be
reachable through the C signature, or the parity test would be asking this side
a question it cannot answer.

**`[vendor]` The pin details are from the pinctrl file for this exact package,
`stm32h7a3z(g-i)txq`:** `usart3_tx_pd8` at AF7 with `bias-pull-up`,
`usart3_rx_pd9` at AF7. The pull-up is applied to the transmit pin alone because
that is what the file specifies, rather than symmetrically because that would
look tidier. The same file gives `fdcan1_rx_pd0` and `fdcan1_tx_pd1` at AF9,
which independently confirms what chapter 9 settled by reasoning when st.com was
unreachable.

## What is still not answered

`[measured]` Which clock USART3 takes is answered to within a ratio, above.
`console_report` prints the assumed clock, the prescaler, the divisor, the
register word, the achieved rate and the error, and then says in as many words
that a legible line confirms the assumption. On Thursday 12 November 2026 that
line was legible, which is why the section above exists.

`[unconfirmed]` What the oscillator's actual frequency is, as opposed to the
ratio between it and this peripheral. Bounding that needs a second clock to
compare against, and nothing on this bench is wired to provide one yet.

`[measured]` **One of the four questions is answered.** `CREL` reads
`0x32141218`, which is M_CAN core release 3.2.1 dated 18 December 2014, read on
Thursday 12 November 2026 by the register map probe. `ENDN` read `0x87654321` in
the same capture, confirming the FDCAN base address on silicon. See
[`register-map.md`](register-map.md).

`[unconfirmed]` **Three remain**, because the probe reads two registers and
configures nothing: the stack high-water mark, whether one `RXF0A` write
releases a whole run of elements, and the loopback itself. The last two need a
configured controller, which needs a bit timing, a message memory layout and a
transceiver. What has changed is that they are reachable rather than blocked:
there is a console, it has been seen to work, and the controller answers.

One figure did come free with the capture. `[measured]` The console's transmit
wait never exceeded 1002 reads against a bound of 100000, steady from the third
line onwards, so that bound carries about a hundredfold margin at this clock.
The bound was chosen by argument; it is now chosen by argument and confirmed by
a run.
